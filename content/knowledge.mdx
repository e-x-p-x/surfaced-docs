Knowledge is the material Surfaced draws on when it writes. It sits under **Workspace settings → Knowledge** (sidebar footer) and has four parts.

| Page | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| [Business profile](/content/business-profile) | What your business does, and the names that count as you. |
| Website content | The pages of your own site that have been read. |
| Documents | Files you upload. |
| [Product catalogue](/content/product-catalogue) | Your product feed, where you have one. |

## Website content

The pages of your site that Surfaced has indexed, listed as **Discovered pages**.

Your own domains are indexed first and get priority on your plan's page allowance, ahead of anything else. "Your own domains" means your brand domain plus every entry in **Owned domains** in [workspace settings](/set-up/workspace), with the `www` variant of each treated as the same site.

This is the second reason to keep Owned domains complete. The first is that a citation to a domain you have not listed does not count toward your [citation rate](/metrics/citation-rate); the second is that the domain's pages are not prioritised for reading either.

Adding an owned domain registers it and starts a crawl from its homepage, within your plan's page allowance.

<Note>
  Removing an owned domain does not delete anything. The site loses its priority and its reserved allowance, and the pages already read are kept.
</Note>

## Documents

Files you upload for the generator to draw on: positioning documents, product sheets, research, anything that states something true about you that your website does not.

This is the right home for material you would otherwise paste into a brief every time.

## How this connects to the rest

Knowledge is an input to writing, not to measurement. Nothing here changes [brand visibility](/metrics/brand-visibility) or any other tracked number on its own, with one exception: the name lists on [Business profile](/content/business-profile), which decide what counts as a mention of you.

What it does change is whether a generated draft is specific. A brief run against a thin knowledge base produces text that reads like it could be about any company in your category, which is the single most common reason a draft needs heavy editing.
